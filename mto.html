1. Stress on a Fluid Element & Newton's Law of Viscosity

Theory

A fluid element in 3D carries normal stresses ($\sigma_{xx},\sigma_{yy},\sigma_{zz}$) acting perpendicular to each face, and shear stresses ($\tau_{ij}$) acting tangential to each face. In $\tau_{ij}$, the first index $i$ denotes the direction of the outward normal of the plane, and the second index $j$ denotes the direction of the stress itself — e.g. $\tau_{yx}$ is the shear stress on a plane whose normal is along $y$, acting in the $x$-direction.

Unlike a solid, which resists a fixed shear load with a fixed (finite) deformation, a fluid continues to deform indefinitely under any sustained shear stress, however small. This continuous deformation is what defines a fluid.

Stress Definitions — Normal & Shear Stress (Limit Definition)

Consider a small force $\delta \mathbf F$ acting on a small area element $\delta A_n$ whose orientation is given by the unit normal $\hat n$. Resolving $\delta \mathbf F$ into a component along $\hat n$ (normal) and a component tangent to the surface (shear), and shrinking the area to a point defines the stress at that point:

On any given plane there is one normal stress and, in 3D, two independent shear components (e.g. on the plane whose normal is $x$: $\sigma_{xx}$, $\tau_{xy}$, $\tau_{xz}$). Repeating this for the $y$- and $z$-normal planes gives the full 3×3 stress array (stress tensor) at a point.

Body Forces vs Surface Forces (General Classification)

All forces on a fluid can be classified as:

  • Body forces — act on the mass/volume of the element without contact, e.g. gravitational force and electromagnetic force. For an element of volume $d\forall$: $dF_B=\rho\,\mathbf g\,d\forall$ (gravitational body force per unit volume $=\rho g$).
  • Surface forces — act by direct contact on the surface of the element, e.g. pressure force, friction, and surface tension.

Classification of Fluids (Theory)

  • Newtonian fluids — shear stress is linearly proportional to shear rate through a constant $\mu$ (e.g. water, air).
  • Non-Newtonian fluids — the shear stress/shear rate relationship is not a single straight line through the origin:
    • Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) — apparent viscosity decreases as shear rate increases (e.g. polymer solutions, blood).
    • Dilatant (shear-thickening) — apparent viscosity increases as shear rate increases (e.g. cornstarch/starch suspension).
    • Bingham/ideal plastic — requires a finite yield stress before flow begins (e.g. toothpaste, drilling mud).
  • Time-dependent fluids — apparent viscosity depends on the duration of shearing, not just its rate:
    • Thixotropic — apparent viscosity decreases with time under a constant applied shear rate (e.g. paints, gels).
    • Rheopectic — apparent viscosity increases with time under a constant applied shear rate (rare; e.g. some gypsum/clay pastes).

Power-Law (Ostwald–de Waele) model: $\tau_{yx}=K\left(\dfrac{du}{dy}\right)^n$, where $K$ is the flow consistency index and $n$ is the flow behaviour index. The apparent viscosity is $\eta = K\left(\dfrac{du}{dy}\right)^{n-1}$. For $n=1$ this reduces to a Newtonian fluid ($K=\mu$); $n<1$ gives shear-thinning behaviour; $n>1$ gives shear-thickening behaviour.

Bingham plastic model: $\tau_{yx}=\tau_y+\mu_p\left(\dfrac{du}{dy}\right)$ — the fluid requires a yield stress $\tau_y$ before it begins to flow, then behaves linearly.

Wetting & Reynolds Number (Theory)

Contact angle $\theta$ describes surface wetting: hydrophilic/wetting surfaces have $\theta<90^\circ$ (liquid spreads); hydrophobic/non-wetting surfaces have $\theta>90^\circ$ (liquid beads up).

$Re$ is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Low $Re\ll1$ → viscous (creeping/laminar) flow dominates; high $Re\gg1$ → inertial/pressure forces dominate, flow tends toward turbulence. Pipe flow is classed as laminar for $Re<2300$, transitional for $23004000$.

Continuum Flow Classification (Theory)

Flows are broadly classified as:

  • Inviscid flow ($\mu\to0$, idealized, frictionless) vs viscous flow (real fluid, friction present). Viscous flow is further split into laminar (smooth, layered) and turbulent (chaotic, mixing) regimes, separated by the no-slip condition at solid boundaries.
  • Compressible (density varies significantly, e.g. gas flow at high speed) vs incompressible (density essentially constant, most liquid flows).
  • Internal flow (bounded by solid walls, e.g. pipe flow) vs external flow (fluid flows over an immersed body, e.g. flow past an aerofoil).
  • Stagnation points — points on a body (commonly labelled A, C, etc.) where the local fluid velocity relative to the body is zero, marking where the approaching flow divides and rejoins.

8. System Conservation Laws & the Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT)

Theory

Conservation laws (mass, momentum, angular momentum, energy, entropy) are naturally stated for a fixed mass of matter — a system. In fluid mechanics it is far more convenient to work with a fixed region of space — a control volume (CV). The Reynolds Transport Theorem is the mathematical bridge between the two viewpoints.

Every extensive property $N$ (mass, momentum, energy, etc.) can be written as an integral of its corresponding intensive property $\eta=dN/dm$ over the system:

Extensive property $N$Intensive property $\eta=dN/dm$
Mass, $m$$1$
Linear momentum, $\mathbf P$$\mathbf V$
Angular momentum, $\mathbf H$$\mathbf r\times\mathbf V$
Total energy, $E$$e=u+V^2/2+gz$
Entropy, $S$$s$

14. Fluid Kinematics — Velocity Field, Material Derivative, Streamlines & Pathlines derivation added

Theory

Differential (kinematic) analysis describes fluid motion point-by-point through a velocity field $\mathbf V(x,y,z,t)=u\,\hat i+v\,\hat j+w\,\hat k$, rather than through integral balances over a whole control volume. A key subtlety is that any fluid property carried by a moving particle changes both because the field itself changes with time at a fixed point, and because the particle is convected to a new point where the field value differs.

Theory

As a fluid element moves, it can simultaneously translate, rotate, stretch (linear strain) and shear (angular strain). Separating the velocity field into these components is the basis of differential fluid-motion analysis and later of the viscous stress terms in the Navier–Stokes equations.

Derivation

Consider two neighbouring points separated by $(dx,dy)$ inside a moving fluid element, with velocity components $u,v$ at the reference point. A first-order Taylor expansion gives the velocity at the neighbouring point as $u+\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}dx+\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}dy$ (similarly for $v$).
